About the workshop

Topics of interest

  • Agentic AI and LLM-driven pipelines
  • Bridging LLM-based and symbolic architectures
  • Service and knowledge graph description, planning and grounding
  • Affordance-based discovery and composition
  • Semantic interoperability and alignment
  • Trust, verification, entailment and closure assumptions
  • Agent protocols and Web-native agents
  • Goal decomposition and human-agent interaction
  • LLM-assisted annotation of tools and services
  • Benchmarks, testbeds and scenarios

The original Semantic Web placed autonomous agents at the centre of a globally distributed, machine-readable knowledge ecosystem. RDF, OWL, SPARQL, and mature reasoning engines emerged alongside extensive work on service description, mediation, and discovery, addressing the same open, multi-agent settings in which agentic AI systems are now being deployed. The Semantic Web Services community explored how agents with different ontological commitments could discover, combine, and invoke one another’s capabilities.

LLM-based agentic AI is now confronting the same challenge. Frameworks such as the Model Context Protocol and Agent2Agent largely describe tools and services in natural language, selecting between them through linguistic similarity rather than formal semantics. This risks reintroducing failure modes that the Semantic Web Services community spent a decade characterising and, in many cases, solving.

SAWA brings together the Multi-Agent Systems, Semantic Web Services, and Semantic Web communities to explore how this research legacy can inform the design of contemporary LLM-based agentic systems. It will critically assess how established research in the Semantic Web and Multi-Agent Systems can inform agent, service, and resource discovery in contemporary LLM-based systems. This includes exploring the potential of affordance-based approaches to complement linguistic descriptions of tools and services, providing a more grounded basis for identifying and selecting capabilities in dynamic, multi-agent environments.

Challenges

Four questions will drive the morning. They also set the scope for lightning talks.

What should an agent be told about a capability?

Formal descriptions such as OWL-S profiles and WSMO capabilities set out a service's inputs and outputs along with its preconditions and effects. Current agent protocols mostly pair natural-language descriptions with structured typing of inputs and outputs. What does formal grounding buy, and what does it cost to author? And how could anyone check that a capability does what its description says?

How should agents discover what they can do here and now?

An affordance is what an agent can do given its current state and the environment it is in, which a static service description cannot express. How do discovery and goal decomposition change when capabilities depend on context?

Where should agreement on meaning happen?

Agents with different ontological commitments still have to understand one another. Should agreement come in advance, through shared ontologies, or at runtime, through alignment and mediation, perhaps with LLMs doing part of the mediating? And what may an agent assume about what a knowledge graph leaves unsaid?

What would make semantics adoptable this time, and how would we know it works?

If the effort of authoring formal descriptions held earlier approaches back, what would a lighter route look like alongside protocols such as MCP and A2A, perhaps with LLMs helping to write and maintain the descriptions? Which benchmarks and testbeds would show that it helps?

Who should come

Researchers working on Semantic Web Services, multi-agent systems, knowledge graphs or agentic AI will find the most to argue about, and so will practitioners building agentic systems in industry. Early-career researchers are especially welcome. We are aiming for a room of 20 to 30 people with a mix of career stages, institutions and countries.
